Five Regions, One Ladder

NEA measures PM2.5 separately for the north, south, east, west and central regions. Every region climbs the same ladder — the wider the averaging window, the smaller the number — but they do not start from the same rung. Right now — is worst and — cleanest, — apart on the 24-hour reading.

PSI band

NEA publishes no boundary file for its five reporting regions — only one label point each. The shapes above are the Voronoi partition of those five points clipped to Singapore's coastline: every place is shaded by the reporting point nearest it. Treat the borders as approximate.

The cards

Five windows per region, all ending at the same instant, all on one shared scale so the cards can be read against each other. The bars are the PSI each window reports. The 1-hour, 24-hour and 365-day figures are set large: the first is the air you are breathing, the second is what NEA publishes, the third is what you have actually been living in.

Method

The 1-hour number

NEA's PM2.5 band table is defined for a 24-hour average. Putting a single hourly reading through it gives a PSI-equivalent, not a PSI. It is the right number for “how bad is it outside right now” and the wrong number to quote against a published PSI.

Why the bars fall

A rolling mean is a low-pass filter. Haze arrives as episodes lasting days against a clean baseline — exactly the signal a long window removes. The 365-day bar is lower because the averaging discarded the episode, not because the air cleared.

The conversion

Segmented linear, NEA Equation 1, breakpoints (0,0) (12,50) (55,100) (150,200) (250,300) (350,400) (500,500). Verified against NEA's own worked example: 40 µg/m³ → 83.

Gaps

Regions drop out during instrument downtime. Each window reports its coverage — the share of its hours that actually carry a reading — in the tooltip, and a window is averaged over the hours it has rather than assuming zero for the rest.
